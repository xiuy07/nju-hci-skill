# User Research and Usability Validation

## Choose evidence for the question

| Question | Starting methods | Unsupported inference |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How does real work happen? | Observation, contextual inquiry | One interface caused every difficulty |
| How do people describe an experience? | Interviews, surveys | Self-report establishes actual behavior |
| Can people complete a task? | Task testing, think-aloud | A small sample represents everyone |
| Is information findable? | Card sorting, tree testing, search tasks | One sorting exercise determines final navigation |
| What might be wrong with a prototype? | Walkthroughs, heuristic evaluation | Expert inference equals measurement |
| Does a design improve an outcome? | Comparative experiments | Non-random changes automatically establish causation |

Quick feedback, controlled testing, field studies, and predictive evaluation organize work and may combine techniques. They are not mutually exclusive categories.

## A DECIDE research brief

Determine goals, explore questions, choose methods, identify practical limitations, decide participant and ethical arrangements, and evaluate and communicate evidence. A sufficient brief states the decision, questions, population, tasks, material versions, measures, recording, and how results will be used.

Recruit for differences relevant to task performance, such as experience, language, devices, and assistive technology. Demographics alone are not a substitute. Qualitative work may iterate in small rounds; quantitative comparisons require precision or power planning. Neither five nor twenty to thirty participants is a universal guarantee.

## Interviews and observation

Ask about recent concrete experiences. Reconstruct goals, choices, obstacles, workarounds, and outcomes. Avoid relying on hypothetical willingness to use a product. Ask one neutral question at a time without embedding your solution. Attend to dominance and conformity in group discussion.

Record time, task, actions, environment, interruptions, and collaboration. Separate observations, participant explanations, and researcher interpretations. Contextual questions clarify meaning but interrupt work; identify resulting influence.

Arrange suitable consent for recordings, allow withdrawal, and collect only relevant information. Use pseudonyms or aggregation in reports. A plan to record does not itself authorize recording.

## Task testing protocol

Use actual or likely users and believable goals without disclosing control names or steps. For example, ask someone to deal with an event they can no longer attend rather than to click a cancellation button.

Explain that the product is being tested, check equipment and material, and pilot ambiguous tasks. Use consistent neutral prompts, record assistance and demonstrations, and stop excessively long or unsuitable tasks when appropriate. Assisted completion is not unassisted success.

Testing may be in-person or remote and moderated or unmoderated. Examine how screen sharing, connectivity, equipment, and privacy affect behavior. Think-aloud helps explain reasoning but can affect timing; use consistent protocols or a separate performance measurement phase.

Define task start, end, success, partial success, errors, help, and timeout. Distinguish inability to complete from technical failure and missing measurement. Retain failed and stopped attempts rather than reporting only successful users' speed.

## Measures and instruments

Map goals to observable signals and then metrics. Combine appropriate measures of success, time, errors, recovery, satisfaction, and workload. HEART suggests Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task success as candidate dimensions. Select relevant dimensions; longer time spent is not automatically better.

Check instrument version, language, items, direction, and scoring. SUS can measure overall perceived usability; NASA-TLX measures workload. They are not interchangeable satisfaction measures. Changing items may invalidate comparability. Explain how individual ordinal items and composite scores are analyzed.

## Analysis and delivery

Preserve observation excerpts and quotations when coding qualitative material. Develop themes, inspect counterexamples, and state sample limitations. Conflicting needs across roles or tasks should remain visible.

For quantitative descriptions, report denominators, valid sample sizes, and reasons for missingness. Use the experimental analysis reference when inference is required. Deliver scope, findings, evidence, design implications, unresolved questions, and the next validation focus rather than a list of quotations alone.
